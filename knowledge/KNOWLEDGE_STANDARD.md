# Knowledge Base Authoring Standard

This standard applies to every domain knowledge base in this repository.

## 1. Scope and decisions
Define the domain and subdomain boundaries, intended users and consuming skills, decisions and tasks supported, and explicit exclusions. Avoid generic encyclopaedic content that is not useful to repository tasks.

## 2. Required knowledge dimensions
Include the dimensions that materially apply:
1. Fundamentals: definitions, concepts, terminology, mental models and principles.
2. Frameworks: recognised models, schools of thought, standards and competing approaches; identify origins and assumptions.
3. Methods: techniques and procedures, prerequisites, sequence, inputs and outputs.
4. Decision criteria: how to select, adapt, compare or reject approaches; include trade-offs.
5. Evidence: what counts as reliable evidence, how to interpret it and validity limits.
6. Practice: worked examples, counterexamples, edge cases and failure modes.
7. Quality: observable characteristics of strong and weak work.
8. Risks and boundaries: ethical, legal, safety, privacy, accessibility and professional limitations.
9. References: traceable sources and review metadata.

Not every domain requires nine separate files. Relevant dimensions must still be covered.

## 3. Explain, do not merely name
Weak: "Use JTBD to understand customers."
Strong knowledge explains the model's purpose, core concepts, assumptions, how to conduct the analysis, interpret evidence, when it is useful, when it is misleading, and how findings influence decisions.

Naming a framework does not demonstrate understanding.

## 4. Framework and method records
For each material framework or method, document as applicable: name and origin, purpose, core concepts, assumptions, application logic, inputs and outputs, appropriate use and contraindications, strengths and weaknesses, known criticisms, repository adaptations and source references.

Distinguish the original framework from this repository's adaptation.

## 5. Source quality and provenance
Prefer, in order appropriate to the domain: primary research, legislation and official standards; peer-reviewed research and systematic reviews; official documentation and recognised professional bodies; established textbooks; reputable practitioner material clearly labelled as such.

For each material source record author/organisation, title, publication or update date, publisher or stable URL/DOI/ISBN, access date for changeable sources, claims supported and known limitations or conflicts of interest.

Never fabricate citations. Unverified sources must be labelled and must not be the sole support for material claims.

## 6. Evidence and uncertainty labels
Distinguish:
- Established: strong converging evidence or authoritative requirements.
- Supported practice: credible professional convention, not necessarily experimentally established.
- Contested: credible disagreement or material limitations.
- Emerging: early evidence or evolving practice.
- Repository recommendation: an explicit design choice made for these skills.
- Project-specific: supplied facts, assumptions or decisions that do not generalise.

These labels do not replace explaining evidence quality.

## 7. Examples and operationalisation
Include examples that show reasoning, not only final answers. Where useful include a worked example with assumptions and evidence, a counterexample, a boundary case, a decision tree or comparison table. Use synthetic data unless real data is authorised and safe to publish.

## 8. Currency and maintenance
Every domain README or source register must state maintainer role, last reviewed date, review cadence or triggers, and known outdated sections or pending research.

Repository defaults: high-change/high-risk domains at least quarterly or on material changes; moderate-change domains every six months; stable foundational material annually or when authoritative sources change. Adjust to actual domain risk.

## 9. Copyright and responsible synthesis
Write original explanations and examples. Attribute ideas, models and sources. Do not reproduce substantial copyrighted text, paid course material or proprietary worksheets. Do not imply endorsement by authors, publishers or standards bodies. Do not let one book or author stand in for a whole discipline.

## 10. How skills consume knowledge
Each skill must identify its knowledge dependencies and portability mode; read relevant knowledge before applying a method; connect concepts to its task workflow; adapt theory to the task rather than dumping generic content; state when evidence is insufficient; and never claim to have consulted inaccessible documents.

Keep concise task-specific essentials in SKILL.md and reusable depth in the knowledge base.

## 11. Review checklist
Reviewers should check scope, conceptual accuracy, framework origins and assumptions, actionable bounded methods, explicit decision criteria, evidence and uncertainty, examples and failure modes, risks and boundaries, traceable sources, skill discoverability and copyright/privacy.

A document that fails material checks remains a draft, not a completed knowledge base.
