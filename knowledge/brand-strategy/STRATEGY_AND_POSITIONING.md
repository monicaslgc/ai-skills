# Strategy and Positioning

**Status:** Pilot module  
**Last reviewed:** 2026-11-19  
**Review cadence:** Annual, and after material market, category or framework changes.

## 1. Strategy is a set of choices

Brand strategy is not a collection of polished statements. It is a connected set of choices about organisational role and ambition; priority audiences and situations; problems addressed; value delivered; capabilities and proof; competitive context; and behaviours, experiences and trade-offs required to deliver the promise.

Strategy should guide product, service, operations, people and communications. If it only changes marketing language, it may be messaging rather than strategy.

Repository recommendation: every material choice records evidence, interpretation, implication, trade-off, confidence and approval state.

## 2. Positioning and its boundaries

Positioning establishes the context in which a defined audience understands an offering, the meaningful value it provides, and how it compares with relevant alternatives.

Distinguish:
- Positioning: strategic choice of audience, competitive context and differentiated value.
- Messaging: how positioning is explained to audiences and situations.
- Tagline: short expressive device; it cannot carry the full positioning logic.
- Value proposition: value created and why it matters; one component of positioning.
- Brand identity: intended system of meaning and expression that communicates and delivers the chosen role.

Difference matters only when relevant to an audience, credible and meaningful against an alternative.

## 3. Audience and decision context

Define an audience in terms that predict needs, behaviour or fit. Demographics may help but are rarely sufficient.

Investigate situation, trigger, context of use, desired outcome, current workaround, pains, risks, constraints, decision criteria, switching costs, ability/willingness to adopt or pay, and disqualifying conditions.

Do not treat everyone who could theoretically use the offer as the target. Prioritisation is a choice.

## 4. Competitive alternatives: the real comparison

Ask what the audience would do if the offering did not exist. Alternatives may include direct products, substitutes, internal tools, spreadsheets, manual work, agencies, hiring staff, existing suppliers, delaying the decision or doing nothing.

Prioritise alternatives using win/loss data, interviews, observed workflows, procurement shortlists and switching behaviour. Desk research can identify plausible alternatives but cannot prove what a particular segment considers.

Comparing only visible direct competitors can create false differentiation. A feature may be unique among vendors but irrelevant if customers prefer a manual workaround.

## 5. From capabilities to differentiated value

Separate three levels:
1. Attribute/capability: what the organisation or offer has or does.
2. Functional consequence: what that capability enables or changes.
3. Customer value: why that consequence matters in the audience's situation.

Synthetic example:
- Capability: repair status updates at each milestone.
- Consequence: customers can see progress without contacting support.
- Value: less uncertainty and less time spent chasing updates.

A capability is not differentiated merely because it is unusual internally. Compare it with real alternatives and establish that the difference matters to the audience.

## 6. Proof and reasons to believe

A reason to believe is evidence making a promise credible. It may include verified capability, documented process/service performance, independently validated certification, customer outcomes with appropriate methodology, demonstrable expertise or infrastructure, and transparent terms or guarantees.

Classify proof:
- Direct evidence: observed/documented evidence of the specific claim.
- Proxy evidence: related evidence that supports but does not prove it.
- Assertion: internal statement not yet substantiated.
- Hypothesis: proposed claim requiring validation.

A feature list does not prove customer outcomes; a testimonial does not establish typical results; a certification does not support claims outside its scope.

For comparative claims such as fastest, only or best, define comparison set, geography, timeframe, metric and method. Otherwise remove or qualify the claim.

## 7. Choose a category frame deliberately

A category gives audiences a mental context: what kind of solution this is, what alternatives to compare, what benefits to expect and sometimes how to evaluate price or risk.

- Existing category: borrows familiar expectations and reduces explanation cost; risks comparison with established incumbents.
- Subcategory/use-case frame: narrows context to clarify value; may limit perceived scope.
- New category: may help when existing categories consistently mislead; requires education, investment and patience.

Choose the frame that makes evidenced value easiest for the best-fit audience to understand while remaining credible and commercially useful. Do not choose a category merely because it sounds impressive.

## 8. Practical positioning workflow

This is a repository synthesis informed by established positioning practice, not a reproduction of one proprietary method.

1. Map real alternatives. Rank by observed frequency or importance; distinguish evidence from speculation.
2. Identify capabilities that matter against those alternatives. Remove differences that are irrelevant or unsubstantiated.
3. Translate capabilities into value. State functional consequence, audience benefit and situation.
4. Identify best-fit segments. Find those who value the benefit, have the problem, can adopt and are likely to choose it. Include poor-fit segments.
5. Select category frame. Compare comprehension, credibility, competitive implications, value clarity and education burden.
6. Form materially different positioning territories. Each should imply a different audience, value emphasis, competitive frame or trade-off.
7. Stress-test relevance, distinctiveness, credibility, durability, focus, feasibility and decision usefulness.
8. State and validate. Write a concise internal positioning statement, explain it plainly, test comprehension and assess whether it changes decisions.

## 9. Positioning statement structure

For [priority audience in a defined situation], [offering] is the [category/frame] that [meaningful value/differentiated outcome], because [credible capability and proof]. Unlike [relevant alternative], it [meaningful distinction].

This is a diagnostic structure, not required public copy. If a component is unknown, mark it as a hypothesis or evidence gap rather than filling it with generic claims.

## 10. Compare options with explicit criteria

| Criterion | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does the audience care about this value in a real decision context? |
| Distinctiveness | Is the difference meaningful against alternatives actually considered? |
| Credibility | Can the organisation prove and consistently deliver it? |
| Focus | Is it clear who is prioritised and who is not? |
| Durability | Can the value remain meaningful as conditions change? |
| Feasibility | Do capabilities, economics, operations and roadmap support it? |
| Comprehension | Does the audience understand category and value? |
| Decision utility | Does it guide product, service, sales and communication choices? |

Scores are structured judgement, not objective truth. Define weights only with the decision owner's agreement. A fatal credibility or legal issue cannot be compensated by a high average.

## 11. Coherence and trade-offs

Test the chain: audience → problem → value → capability → proof → experience.

Ask what will be prioritised, what will not be served or claimed, what capabilities/investments must change, what customers and employees must experience, which existing offers or messages conflict, and what evidence would cause a review. A strategy without trade-offs is often a wish list.

## 12. Failure modes and limits

- Category-first thinking creates labels before understanding value and audience.
- Competitor-only analysis overlooks status quo, workarounds and non-consumption.
- Feature-led differentiation describes the organisation rather than customer value.
- Broad target audiences weaken relevance and prioritisation.
- Unsupported uniqueness creates legal, reputational and trust risk.
- Invented insight converts stakeholder assumptions into false evidence.
- Overfitting to current customers can ignore future ambition; separate evidence from aspiration.
- Static positioning becomes stale as products, markets and alternatives change.
- Framework worship substitutes models for research and judgement.

Positioning methods do not guarantee market success. Validate hypotheses through customer research, sales evidence, product usage and commercial outcomes.

## 13. Synthetic worked example

A fictional repair company considers:
- A: Premium repair craftsmanship. Relevant if customers value specialist quality and the company can prove it.
- B: Repair without uncertainty. Potentially relevant if customers struggle with opaque status and delays; credibility requires reliable updates and honest commitments.
- C: Cheapest repair. Defensible only if cost leadership is evidenced, sustainable and strategically desirable; it may create margin pressure.

Before selecting B, verify the pain, test audience comprehension, demonstrate operational capability and compare actual alternatives. This example does not establish B as universally best.
