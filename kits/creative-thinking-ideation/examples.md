# Creative Thinking & Ideation examples

All scenarios below are fictional. They illustrate expected behaviour, not validated outcomes.

## Example 1: Collaborative back-and-forth

**User:** I want a better way for people to remember the small things they learn from each other.

**Good assistant move:** "The interesting part may be less about storing knowledge and more about keeping a human connection attached to it. What if each shared tip carried a tiny story about when it mattered? We could explore a personal-memory direction and a community-ritual direction."

Why it works: it identifies a creative spark, adds a development and opens two branches without deciding.

## Example 2: Creative Room

**Challenge:** Make a neighbourhood repair event more inviting.

- **Visionary:** What if it felt like a festival of fixing rather than a service desk?
- **Artist:** Give each repaired object a small before/after story and a visible trace of its history.
- **Inventor:** Pair visitors with a repair mentor for a ten-minute micro-session.
- **Challenger:** Does the event feel intimidating because people think they must bring something broken? Could observation-only participation be welcome?
- **Synthesizer:** Combine the festival framing, visible stories and low-pressure observation into a welcoming event journey.

Why it works: perspectives contribute different mechanisms and frames.

## Example 3: Divergence without premature ranking

**Challenge:** Help a small team share progress without adding more meetings.

Possible directions:
1. A visual asynchronous wall of evidence.
2. A rotating "what changed?" audio note.
3. A shared map of blockers and dependencies.
4. A lightweight weekly demo exchange.
5. A decision diary that captures only consequential changes.
6. A team signal that invites help when work is stuck.
7. A rotating open studio hour with no status reporting.
8. A narrative changelog organised around outcomes.

Do not claim one is best without criteria or evidence.

## Example 4: Challenge the brief

**User:** We need a chatbot to improve customer support.

**Working reframe:** "We want customers to resolve common issues with less effort, while preserving access to a person when judgement or empathy is needed."

The reframe opens possibilities beyond a chatbot: clearer self-service, better forms, proactive guidance, improved agent tools or a hybrid experience.

## Example 5: Productive disagreement

**User:** Let's add points and leaderboards to make the experience engaging.

**Good assistant move:** "That could create momentum, but it may reward activity rather than meaningful progress. What if we kept the sense of progress but made it personal—visible growth, milestones or a collection of completed work, without ranking people?"

Why it works: it challenges the mechanism and offers a constructive alternative.


## Example 6: A purposeful Creative Room round

**Challenge:** A small museum wants younger visitors to engage with a collection without turning every exhibit into a screen experience.

**Round objective:** Find a new frame for participation.

- **Artist:** Treat the collection as a set of unfinished stories visitors can interpret, not just objects to read about.
- **Inventor:** Let visitors leave a question or observation that becomes part of a rotating interpretation layer.
- **Challenger:** Are we assuming engagement must be visible or measurable during the visit?
- **Interaction:** The Inventor's contribution responds to the Artist's story frame; the Challenger questions whether a public contribution is necessary.

**Carry-forward:** Explore quiet, private forms of participation alongside visible contribution.

## Example 7: Productive tension

**Tension:** A workshop should feel spontaneous, but the organisers need predictable timing.

**What each side protects:** spontaneity protects discovery and energy; timing protects access, staffing and participant confidence.

**Possible moves:** keep the opening and close fixed while leaving the middle modular; offer parallel activities with different durations; make the schedule visible but allow participants to move between stations.

No option is selected until the user chooses which value to protect most.

## Example 8: Analogy bridge

**Challenge:** Make a complex service easier to understand.

**Source pattern:** A museum audio guide lets people choose depth without changing the underlying exhibition.

**Transfer:** Offer progressive layers of explanation—quick orientation, practical detail and deeper context—without forcing everyone through the same sequence.

**Limit:** A service may require decisions or actions that an exhibition does not; information layering alone may not solve those needs.

## Example 9: Constraint remix

**Challenge:** Design a small community exhibition.

**Fixed:** venue safety and access requirements. **Assumed:** every item needs a dedicated display surface. **Chosen:** the exhibition lasts one weekend.

**Remix:** Remove the assumption that every item must be displayed physically. Explore a listening station, a guided route, or a shared table where objects appear in timed sequences. Restore the fixed requirements before treating any direction as viable.


## Example 10: Converge without flattening

**Challenge:** A team has explored ways to make a public workshop more welcoming.

**Patterns:** low-pressure participation; visible shared learning; flexible levels of involvement.

**Possible combination:** pair an optional observation route with short mentor-led activities and a shared wall of discoveries. The combination connects access, participation and collective learning.

**Outlier to preserve:** a quiet, self-guided route for visitors who do not want to interact. It addresses a different need and should not be absorbed into the group activity.

**Next move:** ask which direction the user wants to develop. Do not rank the ideas by frequency.

## Example 11: Develop a concept

**Starting idea:** A neighbourhood repair event should feel more like a festival than a service desk.

**Concept in one sentence:** A community gathering where repairing objects becomes a shared, visible act of learning and storytelling.

**Core experience:** Visitors can observe, bring an item for help, join a short repair lesson or share the story of an object. Participation has several levels; nobody is required to present or repair something.

**Design principles:** welcoming before instructional; visible learning; respect for objects and their histories; low-pressure participation.

**Unknowns:** available repair expertise, venue requirements, material safety, accessibility and whether visitors prefer demonstrations or hands-on participation.

**First question to test:** Which participation mode feels most inviting to the intended community?

This is a concept seed, not a validated event plan.
