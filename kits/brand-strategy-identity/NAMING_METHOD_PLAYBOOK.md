# Naming Method Playbook

This is a practical, original synthesis of established naming principles. It is informed by the naming and brand-identity literature available to the project team, including Rob Meyerson's *Brand Naming*, Jeremy Miller's *Brand New Name*, Brad Flowers' *The Naming Book* and Alina Wheeler's *Designing Brand Identity*.

This file summarizes methods and principles in our own words. It does not reproduce book passages, exercises verbatim, or substitute for reading the source works.

## 1. Start with strategy, not wordplay

Before generating names, establish:
- What is being named: corporate/umbrella brand, product, service, feature or campaign.
- What the name must enable now and in the future.
- Priority audiences, markets, languages and pronunciation contexts.
- Category conventions and competitor naming patterns.
- Brand personality, intended associations and associations to avoid.
- Mandatory constraints: domains, jurisdictions, legal review, spelling, length, budget and timing.

Write a concise naming brief and distinguish approved facts from hypotheses. A name does not need to describe the offer literally; it must be strategically appropriate and capable of accumulating meaning through the brand experience.

## 2. Generate divergent territories

Do not default to one formula or repeatedly combine obvious category keywords. Explore several distinct routes:
- Descriptive: states what the offer is.
- Suggestive: hints at a benefit, effect or experience.
- Metaphorical: borrows an image or idea from another domain.
- Evocative: creates a mood or association without literal description.
- Founder, place or origin-based: only when appropriate and sustainable.
- Real-word or unexpected-word: uses a familiar word in a distinctive context.
- Coined or constructed: creates a new, natural-sounding word.
- Compound or blend: combines elements only when the result sounds effortless and is easy to understand.

Use semantic territories, word families, concrete imagery, metaphors, sounds, roots, associations and lateral prompts. Generate broadly before judging. Say constructed candidates aloud while creating them. Avoid over-engineered portmanteaux that require a long explanation to justify the spelling or meaning.

## 3. Evaluate the name as a spoken and remembered object

For every candidate, test:
- **Sayability:** can people pronounce it on first sight?
- **Hearability:** can someone understand it when heard once?
- **Spellability:** can someone write it after hearing it?
- **Recall:** can someone remember it later without a prompt?
- **Distinctiveness:** does it stand apart from competitors and category clichés?
- **Adaptability:** can it stretch across future products, services, audiences and markets?
- **Associative fit:** does it create useful associations without over-describing or misleading?
- **Visual form:** is it legible, balanced and recognisable in text and at small sizes?
- **Cross-language risk:** are there awkward sounds, meanings or unintended associations in priority languages?

Do not treat a name's etymology or clever construction as proof that audiences will understand it. A rationale can support a name, but should not be required to rescue it.

## 4. Use practical verbal tests

Run these with people representative of the intended language and audience:
1. **First-sight test:** show the name briefly; ask them to pronounce it.
2. **Dictation test:** say the name once; ask them to spell it.
3. **Delayed-recall test:** return later and ask what they remember.
4. **Association test:** ask what kind of organisation or experience they expect.
5. **Conversation test:** ask them to use it naturally in a sentence and recommend it to someone.
6. **Architecture test:** place it in real use, e.g. [Name] Platform, [Name] Consulting, [Name] Development.
7. **Competitive shelf test:** compare it beside relevant competitors, substitutes and adjacent-category names.

Record actual responses, sample/context and limitations. Informal tests are directional, not statistically representative market research.

## 5. Score consistently, then apply hard gates

Use a project-specific scorecard. A useful default for early comparison is:
- Distinctiveness: 25%
- Pronunciation and hearability: 20%
- Memorability and recognition: 20%
- Sound and personality: 15%
- Strategic flexibility: 10%
- Spelling simplicity: 10%

Adjust weights only when the brief justifies it. Score candidates against observable criteria, not personal taste. Preserve dissent and record why a candidate advances or is rejected.

A high creative score cannot compensate for a failed mandatory domain, material legal collision or serious linguistic problem. Availability and legal checks are separate gates, not just scorecard dimensions.

## 6. Keep stages separate

1. Strategy and naming brief.
2. Broad generation across multiple territories.
3. Quick elimination of obvious weak or unsuitable candidates.
4. Mandatory domain/company/trademark preliminary screen.
5. Linguistic, cultural, pronunciation and competitive-confusion validation.
6. Audience testing of a small shortlist.
7. Qualified legal review and final owner decision.
8. Secure domains/handles and establish the naming architecture after approval.

Do not invest in detailed stories, taglines, logos or identity mockups for candidates that have not passed the required viability gate. Never call a domain or trademark available unless the relevant source explicitly confirms it; unknown means unknown.

## 7. Present names in context

Do not send a raw list and ask people to vote. Present a small, curated set after explaining the brief and evaluation criteria. Give each candidate:
- exact spelling and a simple pronunciation guide;
- one concise strategic rationale and intended associations;
- what makes it distinctive;
- potential weaknesses or ambiguity;
- validation status, with evidence date and confidence;
- how it works in the product/service architecture.

Say every name aloud. Ask reviewers to respond to the name itself before revealing a long backstory. Capture individual reactions before group discussion to reduce conformity. The authorised owner makes the decision; broad consensus is not a substitute for accountability.
