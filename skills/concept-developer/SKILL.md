# Concept Developer

## Purpose
Develop an idea beyond a catchy sentence into a concept that another person can understand, discuss, test or hand off—without pretending it is already validated or fully specified.

This is a reusable model-invoked capability. It develops the concept, not a complete business case, product requirements document or delivery plan.

## Inputs
Use the idea, session history and constraints already supplied. If one missing detail would materially change the concept, ask one focused question. Otherwise state a provisional assumption and continue.

## Procedure
1. **Find the core spark.** State what makes the idea interesting and what should not be lost during development.
2. **Clarify the intended change.** Describe who or what the concept is for, the context in which it matters and the outcome it hopes to create. Do not invent a target audience if none is known.
3. **Define the concept.** Explain the central proposition in plain language: what it is, how it works at a high level and why it is meaningfully different from the starting point.
4. **Describe the experience or mechanism.** Show the key steps, interaction, components or narrative arc. Stay at concept level; do not jump into implementation detail unless requested.
5. **Set concept principles.** Identify 3–5 qualities that should guide future decisions and protect the original intent.
6. **Expose the edges.** Separate fixed constraints, assumptions, dependencies, risks and unknowns. Mark speculative elements clearly.
7. **Offer variations.** When useful, present 2–3 distinct expressions of the same concept (for example, minimal, ambitious or unconventional) without diluting the core.
8. **Define what would make it learnable.** Suggest the most important question or smallest useful experiment, but do not claim the concept is proven.
9. **Return ownership.** Ask what feels right, what feels wrong and what the user wants to change before treating the concept as stable.

## Concept brief
Use a lightweight brief, adapting it to the context:
- **Working name** (optional; never mistake it for a final brand name)
- **Concept in one sentence**
- **Core spark / insight**
- **Intended audience or context** (only when known)
- **Desired change or value**
- **How it works / experience**
- **Key elements**
- **Design principles**
- **Constraints**
- **Assumptions and unknowns**
- **Potential tensions or risks**
- **First question to test**
- **Open decisions**

## Guardrails
- Do not invent research, customer quotes, demand, market evidence or feasibility.
- Do not add AI, automation, apps, gamification or technology by default.
- Preserve unusual or emotionally important aspects even if they are hard to operationalise.
- Distinguish the concept from an implementation, feature list, campaign plan or PRD.
- Do not turn assumptions into requirements.
- Do not claim legal, technical, commercial or operational approval.
- Keep the brief proportionate; not every idea needs every section.
- The user decides when a concept is ready to hand off.

## Acceptance checks
- The central idea and its creative spark remain intact.
- A reader can explain the concept back in their own words.
- The mechanism or experience is concrete enough to discuss.
- Assumptions, constraints and unknowns are visible.
- It is not presented as validated or implementation-ready.
- The user retains final say over the concept.
