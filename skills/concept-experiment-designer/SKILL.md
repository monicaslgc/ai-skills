# Concept Experiment Designer

## Purpose
Turn a critical concept assumption into a low-cost learning experiment. Focus on learning about an early idea or experience, not optimising an established operational process.

## Inputs
Use the concept brief, evaluation and constraints already supplied. If the target audience, critical assumption or decision is unclear and materially affects experiment design, ask one focused question. Otherwise label a provisional assumption.

## Procedure
1. **Name the decision.** State what the experiment should help the user decide and what remains outside its scope.
2. **Select a critical assumption.** Choose one assumption whose failure would materially change the concept. Explain why it matters.
3. **Write a falsifiable learning question.** Avoid vague goals such as "see if people like it". Focus on observable understanding, behaviour, need, willingness to participate, or a specific constraint.
4. **Choose the smallest credible method.** Consider concept interviews, storyboard walkthroughs, paper prototypes, clickable mock-ups, service role-play, landing-page comprehension tests, concierge trials or a limited pilot. Explain what the method can and cannot establish.
5. **Define participants and ethics.** Describe the relevant participant profile without inventing sample access. Address consent, accessibility, privacy, power dynamics and any safety considerations.
6. **Specify the protocol.** Define stimulus, neutral prompts, tasks, observation notes, sample rationale and practical steps. Avoid leading questions and collecting unnecessary personal data.
7. **Set signals and interpretation rules.** Define observable positive, negative and ambiguous signals before the test. Label thresholds as proposed unless supplied or approved. Include disconfirming evidence and likely confounders.
8. **Set boundaries.** Define duration, effort, budget if known, approval needs, stop conditions and what the experiment must not do. Do not expose participants to an unapproved or unsafe live service.
9. **Plan the learning review.** Explain how findings will update the concept, its assumptions and the next decision. Distinguish what the experiment can answer from what remains unknown.

## Output contract
- **Decision and learning question**
- **Critical assumption**
- **Experiment method and rationale**
- **Participants and recruitment assumptions**
- **Stimulus / prototype needed**
- **Protocol and neutral prompts**
- **Signals and interpretation rules**
- **Ethics, privacy, accessibility and safety**
- **Scope, approvals and stop conditions**
- **How findings change the next decision**

## Guardrails
- An experiment is not proof of broad market demand unless its design supports that inference.
- Do not fabricate participants, recruitment channels, sample sizes, thresholds or results.
- Do not treat clicks, stated intent or compliments as equivalent to sustained behaviour.
- Prefer the smallest experiment that can answer the decision-relevant question, but do not call a weak proxy definitive.
- Do not run experiments on real users, publish pages, contact customers, spend money or change production systems without explicit approval.
- Distinguish concept tests from operational improvement experiments, software acceptance tests and formal research.
- Use synthetic data for examples; never request confidential or sensitive participant information unnecessarily.

## Acceptance checks
- One critical assumption and a falsifiable learning question are clear.
- Method is proportionate and its limits are explicit.
- Signals include disconfirming or ambiguous outcomes.
- Participant protections and approval gates are addressed.
- No results or evidence are invented.

## Example prompt
"Design the smallest useful experiment to test the riskiest assumption in this concept. Give me the protocol, neutral questions, success and failure signals, and what we can legitimately conclude."
