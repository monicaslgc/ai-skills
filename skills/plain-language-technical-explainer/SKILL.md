# Plain-language technical explainer

Make a technical matter understandable enough to decide on, without making it wrong.

## Inputs to collect

Ask for what is missing, or mark it as unknown:
- The technical facts: what happened or what is proposed, the cause if known, the limits and the uncertainty.
- The audience: their role, what they already know, what decision or action is expected from them.
- The impact on them: time, cost, risk, customers, deadlines.
- Constraints on the message: channel, length, tone and anything that cannot be shared.

## Workflow

1. Decide the one thing the reader must take away and the decision or action you need from them.
2. Lead with the answer: what it means for them in one or two sentences, before any explanation.
3. Explain only as much of the "why" as helps them decide. Use their words and the examples they know; define any term you cannot avoid in one short phrase.
4. Describe impact in their terms: what works, what does not, who is affected, for how long, what the risk is and what is not yet known.
5. When there is a choice, show two or three options side by side with cost, time, risk and what each gives up, then give a recommendation and the reason.
6. Be exact about uncertainty: say what is confirmed, what is likely and what is unknown, and when you will know more.
7. Say what you need from them and by when, and what happens next on your side and when they will hear from you.
8. Check accuracy: compare the simple version with the technical facts and remove any simplification that changes the meaning. Use analogies only when they do not mislead.
9. Offer two lengths: a short message they can read in half a minute, and a one-page version with the options and the detail. Keep a glossary for repeat topics.

## Rules

- Never hide bad news; lead with it and with the plan.
- Do not blame people or teams; describe causes and fixes.
- Do not promise what is not committed; separate estimates from commitments.
- Keep confidential and personal details out unless the audience needs them and may see them.

## Output format

```
Key message (one or two sentences) | decision or action needed | by when
What it means for you
Options: option | what you get | cost | time | risk | what it gives up
Recommendation and reason
Confirmed | likely | unknown | when we will know
What happens next and when you will hear from us
One-page detail and glossary
```

## Quality checks

- The first lines say what it means for the reader and what is needed from them.
- Options are compared on the same measures, with a recommendation.
- Uncertainty is stated, not hidden.
- A technical colleague agrees the simple version is still correct.
